Conditioned self, mindfulness, and kammic force Esala – Poya Thoughts by Dr Channa Ratnatunga Human Beings possess behavioural tendencies 
that have evolved to ensure their survival and reproduction. The latter ensures, evolutionarily-survival of the species. Besides the 
innate genetic reflexes that preserve an individual life, primordial conditioning for survival and reproduction is intrinsic to all humans, 
arising from evolutionary neurobiology inherited from our early vertebrate and mammalian ancestors (See Fig 1). This instinctive behaviour 
is further shaped by family, culture, and tribal or social conditioning by a process named-neuroplasticity. The latter is a process by which 
the brain keeps adapting to experience – a survival ploy ingrained in the brains of humans. The result is the Conditioned Self—a unique 
personality that develops within the brain’s neural networks from childhood to old age. It represents a continuous process of adaptation 
through experience, enabling the individual to survive and flourish in changing environments. From a Buddhist perspective, this conditioned 
process gives rise to Bhava Taṇhâ (the craving for continued existence), whose principal actors are the sense of ‘I’ and the narrative self. 
Neuroscience suggests that many aspects of this narrative self are associated with the brain’s Default Mode Network (DMN). The DMN (See Fig 2), 
centred primarily in the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and lateral parietal cortex, is involved in self-referential 
thinking, autobiographical memory, mental time travel e.g. ruminating on past ‘baggage’, and the construction of personal identity. While the 
DMN is not the sole neural basis of the self, it appears to play a central role in maintaining the continuous narrative that we experience as ‘me’. 
In contrast, the Central Executive Network (CEN), centred in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (See Fig 3), and interconnected with several cortical 
and subcortical regions, becomes dominant during focused attention and goal-directed tasks. Sustained mindful awareness—”living in the present 
moment”—is thought to reflect greater engagement of this network. Through its interactions with emotional and memory systems, including pathways 
such as the Uncinate Fasciculus, in this way, the CEN contributes to the regulation of emotional reactivity and habitual patterns of thought. 
When mindfulness becomes a stable characteristic rather than a temporary mental state, it develops into a lasting trait. Intensive practice of 
Vipassanâ Bhâvanâ appears capable of producing enduring structural and functional changes in the brain through neuroplasticitic mechanisms and, 
possibly, epigenetic regulation of gene expression within neurons. Although research into these epigenetic mechanisms is still evolving, 
accumulating evidence suggests that meditation can influence biological pathways associated with stress regulation, inflammation, and neural 
plasticity. As this trait matures, habitual survival-driven reactions of the Conditioned Self become progressively weaker. Consequently, 
intentions (Cetanâ) and volitional formations (Saṅkhâra) are less likely to arise from greed, hatred, and delusion, thereby reducing the 
generation of unwholesome Kamma. According to Buddhist philosophy, when craving and ignorance (avijjâ) cease, the accumulation of Kamma capable 
of producing future rebirth also ceases, leading ultimately to liberation from Saṃsâra and hence sorrow. This raises a broader philosophical question. 
Could the Kammic process itself be understood as one of nature’s mechanisms for sustaining the continuity of life-forms in the universe? Survival 
is the fundamental characteristic of all living organisms. In Buddhist terminology, this corresponds broadly to Bhava Taṇhâ—the craving for continued 
existence. If Saṃsâra is accepted as a reality, one may ask whether dependent origination (Paṭicca-samuppâda) describes not merely the continuation 
of individual existence but also a universal process through which life continually renews itself.
